Practical 3 · Secure & Ship

The bank works and it has a front door. It also lets anyone move anyone's money, and it tells nobody when it does. This practical fixes both. You will put a real login in front of the bank, decide in one annotation who may do what, wire up the CDI features you read about in Sessions 11 and 12 — a configuration value, two notifiers behind one interface, an interceptor, and an event that waits for the commit — and then look honestly at what packaging the whole thing as an EAR would change.

Part 1 · What you are building

Two things are wrong with the bank as Practical 2 left it, and they are the two things that separate a coursework exercise from something you would let a stranger near.

The first is that there is no idea of a person. The page, the servlet, the remote interface and the queue all reach the same AccountService, and it will move money out of any account for anyone who asks. There is no who anywhere in the application.

The second is that nothing happens after a transfer except the transfer. In a real bank, moving £25 is not one event, it is one event and four consequences: the customer is emailed, the customer is texted, the branch's audit trail is written, and somebody's fraud rule takes a look. Practical 2's code would have to call all four by hand, from inside the transfer, and know about every one of them.

So this practical adds two ideas, and a third that is really a question about shape:

In plain words

Authentication is proving who you are. A user name and a password. A fingerprint. A card and a PIN. The point is that it is a claim you have to back up — saying "I am Ada" is not authentication; saying "I am Ada and here is the thing only Ada knows" is.

Authorization is a different question, asked afterwards: now that we know who you are, what may you do? Ada may move her own money. The branch manager may see everybody's. Those are two separate decisions and mixing them up is the single most common security bug there is.

In plain words

A role is a name for a group of permissions, so that you can write rules about people you have never met. You do not write "Ada may see the audit log". You write "a BANK_MANAGER may see the audit log", and then you say Ada is one. When a new manager joins, the rule does not change — one row in a table does.

🛂
The analogy: the bank gets a door

Practical 2's bank had a lobby, a counter, and clerks — and no door. Anyone who walked in off the street could ask a clerk to move money.

This practical installs an entrance with a person on it (the login form), a list of who is allowed in and what they are (the bank_users table), a printed rulebook by each clerk's desk saying which jobs require which badge (@RolesAllowed), and a bell on the wall that rings in the back office whenever a job is finished (the event).

And notice the bit that is easy to miss: the rulebook is separate from the clerks. The clerk who can move money does not know or care who you are. Somebody at the door checked your badge, and the container compares it to the rulebook before the clerk is even woken up. That separation is why the security in this practical is four annotations and not four hundred lines.

Part 3 · A login that actually logs you in


  
This is the part of the practical where the course earns its keep, so read this next paragraph
    carefully — it is the single most commonly broken thing in all of Jakarta Security.


  
🚨
    
The half of the configuration that is easy to leave out
      
Session 13 shows you @CustomFormAuthenticationMechanismDefinition and
        @DatabaseIdentityStoreDefinition. Those two annotations describe a complete vault: a
        door, and a list of who is allowed through it.

      
And with that pair alone, nobody can ever log in. Not "it is insecure" — it does
        not work. No exception, no warning, no log line. The form submits, the page redisplays, and the
        user is exactly as anonymous as before.

      
The reason is a difference you cannot see from the annotation. The standard form
        mechanism, @FormAuthenticationMechanismDefinition, makes the container perform the
        login for you: it publishes a j_security_check endpoint and does the work. The
        custom one hands that job back to you. Nothing happens unless your own code asks.

      
So this practical ships LoginBean — the code that asks. It contains the one line
        that makes the whole thing function, and the file the course's Session 13 page does not show you.

    

  


  
Open lab/practical-3-secure-ship/app/src/main/java/com/globalbank/security/LoginBean.java
    and find this. Everything else in the class exists to produce those three arguments:


  
Credential credential =
        new UsernamePasswordCredential(username, new Password(password));

AuthenticationParameters params =
        AuthenticationParameters.withParams().credential(credential);

AuthenticationStatus status =
        securityContext.authenticate(request, response, params);


  
⚠️
    
Read the third argument, and then read it again
      
It is not the credential. authenticate() takes an
        AuthenticationParameters — a small wrapper whose job is to carry the credential
        plus two switches: "is this a fresh login or a re-authentication?" and "should this be
        remembered?". Writing it from memory as
        authenticate(request, response, credential) does not compile, and the error message
        ("Credential cannot be converted to AuthenticationParameters") is not obvious about
        what to do instead.

      
This is a fine moment to learn how to settle an API question rather than guess at it. The Jakarta
        EE API jar is already on your disk in your Maven repository, and the JDK ships a tool that prints
        signatures:

      
javap -cp jakarta.jakartaee-api-10.0.0.jar jakarta.security.enterprise.SecurityContext

      
One second, and you have the real signature in front of you. Reading the API is faster than being
        wrong — and it is a habit worth having for the rest of your career.

Part 4 · Who is allowed to do what


  
You are signed in. Now the second question — and the first thing to understand about it is that
    answers live in three different places, because there are three different things being
    asked.


  

    
The questionWhere the answer goesWhy there

    
      

        May an anonymous visitor reach this URL?
        web.xml's <security-constraint>
        It is a property of the web application, not of any class — and it has to be answered before
          any of your code runs
      

      

        May this caller invoke this bean method?
        @RolesAllowed on the method
        The container checks it at the EJB boundary, before the method body executes, whatever route
          the call came in by
      

      

        Should this page section be shown?
        rendered="#{…}" in the Facelet
        Presentation. It decides what is drawn, and it is not a security check
      

    
  


  
🔑
    
The rule that catches everybody: there is no deny-by-default
      
On a bean that has security annotations anywhere — on the class or on any method — a
        method with no @RolesAllowed, @PermitAll or
        @DenyAll is unchecked. Reachable by anybody, including a caller with
        no identity at all.

      
It is not "denied because nobody said allow". It is "allowed because nobody said deny". This
        surprises almost everyone, and it means that the dangerous operation is the one you
        forgot, not the one you wrote.

      
That is why every public method in AccountService, AuditService and
        RawAccountDao carries an annotation — including the ones that are obviously meant
        to be open. @PermitAll on transferOrRefuse behaves identically to no
        annotation at all, and means something completely different to the next person reading the file.

      and what specifically makes it safe
      
Read that row again and it should worry you. There is a method that moves money, on a bank, open
        to anyone.

      
Three things make it acceptable, and all three have to be true:

      
    
        
  1. It is not on AccountServiceRemote. A remote caller cannot reach
          it — the interface is the list of what the outside world may ask for, and this is not on it.
    2. 
        
  2. It is not on the web page and not on the servlet. Nothing a browser can do
          reaches it.
    3. 
        
  3. Its only caller is TransferMDB, which runs on a container thread with no user, no
          session and no request — so there is no identity for a role check to test against.
    4. 
      

      
And the reason the code was restructured for this — a new
        private moveMoney(…) that both entry points call — is so that the two doors cannot
        accidentally inherit each other's rules. In Practical 2 there was only one door and the question
        never came up. Here it does, and "the check is on the other method" is exactly the kind of thing
        that stops being true six months later.

    

  


  
⚠️
    
The first thing that breaks when you add security to a working application
      
Practical 2's LedgerInitializer creates the three demo accounts with
        em.persist(…), straight to the database. The moment you see that
        AccountService.open() carries @RolesAllowed("CUSTOMER"), the obvious
        tidy-up is to rewrite those three lines to call it — the service already validates the holder name
        and the opening balance, so you would delete duplicated code rather than keep it.

      
Do that, and the application will not start.

      
LedgerInitializer is @Singleton @Startup: it runs while the application is
        deploying. At that moment there is no request, no session, and no signed-in user. The container
        compares nobody's roles against a rule that requires CUSTOMER, refuses the
        call, the @Startup bean fails — and the whole deployment fails with it, before a single
        page can be served.

      
So the rule, and it generalises well beyond this project: setup code runs before there is
        anybody to authorize, and must therefore never call secured code. Seeding goes to the
        EntityManager directly. LedgerInitializer keeps its three
        persist calls, and the duplicated validation stops looking like laziness the moment you
        know why it is there.

      
This is worth dwelling on for a second reason. Nothing about LedgerInitializer changed
        except an annotation on a different class in a different package — and that was enough to
        break it. Security annotations are not local: they change what the whole application is allowed to
        do, including code you were not thinking about when you added them.

Part 5 · CDI: one value, two notifiers, one interceptor


  
Sessions 11 and 12 describe CDI's features one at a time. This part shows them doing a job in a real
    application, which is a different experience — because the interesting question is never "what does
    @Produces do", it is "why would I want one".


  
One value, in one place


  
Search the project's Java for the string "GlobalBank". It appears in exactly one
    place — BankConfiguration — and yet the bank's name is printed by the page header, by the
    audit interceptor and by both notifiers:


  
@Produces @BankName
String bankName = "GlobalBank";


  
Three different classes write bankName in their code and none of them knows where the
    value comes from. They do not look it up, read a properties file, or hold a copy. They each declare
    what kind of thing they need, and CDI hands it to them.


  
⚠️
    
The exception, and it is worth noticing rather than tidying away
      
Open login.xhtml and search it for GlobalBank. You will find
        <h1>GlobalBank</h1> — spelled out in the markup, not injected.
        login-error.xhtml does the same in its heading and its title.

      
So the claim "one place" is true of the Java and false of the project as a whole, and this
        practical does not pretend otherwise. Change the producer to "Ada's Bank" and the bank
        page, the interceptor and the notifiers all follow — while the sign-in page and the failure page
        still say GlobalBank.

      
Why leave it? Because the sign-in page is rendered before anybody is signed in, and it is
        deliberately kept standing on its own: it needs no backing bean beyond the one holding the two
        fields, and it must keep working even if something else in the application is broken. Depending on
        it for a display string is a coupling that buys very little.

      
The general lesson is the one worth keeping: a producer removes duplication in code, not
        duplication in markup. A hard-coded string in a Facelet is a copy, and copies drift —
        which is exactly why the step-2 exercise below asks you to change the value and then go and
        look at every page, rather than trusting that they agreed.

    

  


  
In plain words
    
A producer is a method or field whose job is to make something injectable. The
      container reads @Produces and treats whatever comes out as a thing that can be injected
      — including types it could never have built itself, like String or int.
      CDI cannot construct a String; it has no idea which string you want. So you tell it.

  


  
The project has one of each shape, on purpose:


  

    
@Produces field@Produces method

    
      
HerebankNameauditDepth()

      
Use it whenthe value is simply knownthe value has to be worked out

      
Example= "GlobalBank"read a system property, validate it, fall back to a default

      
Bonus—A method can take parameters, so a producer can depend on other injected things

    
  


  
🚨
    
Neither producer has a scope annotation — and that is not an oversight
      
When a bean has a normal scope — @ApplicationScoped, @RequestScoped,
        anything except @Dependent — CDI hands injection points a proxy: a
        stand-in object that looks up the real instance on each call. That is how a request-scoped bean can
        be injected into an application-scoped one without the wrong instance leaking across requests.

      
And a proxy works by subclassing. String is final, and
        int is a primitive. Neither can be subclassed.

      
So writing

      
@ApplicationScoped @Produces @BankName String bankName = "GlobalBank";

      
is not a warning you can ignore. It is a definition error: CDI refuses to deploy
        the application, and the message says something about proxying that you will only understand once
        you have read this paragraph. Try it once, on purpose — step 4 below walks you through it — and you
        will recognise that error for the rest of your career.

    

  


  
Two beans, one interface, no if


  
The bank now tells customers about transfers by email and by SMS. Both are simulations — they print
    a line — because the point is the wiring, not the mail server.


  
public interface Notifier {
    void send(String message);
}


  
There are two implementations, and both are perfectly good candidates for
    @Inject Notifier. So which one gets injected?


  
🔑
    
The answer is: neither. The application will not deploy.
      
Two beans that both satisfy one injection point is an ambiguous dependency, and
        CDI refuses to guess. You get a deployment error naming both candidates — which is
        genuinely helpful, and is how you find this class of problem quickly.

      
The fix is not an if in the calling code, and it is not a @Named string
        looked up at run time. It is a qualifier: a custom annotation that acts as a
        label, so that the injection point can say which one it means.

    

  


  
A qualifier is a tiny annotation. Here is @Email in full — @Sms is the same
    file with two words changed:


  
@Qualifier
@Retention(RetentionPolicy.RUNTIME)
@Target({ElementType.TYPE, ElementType.FIELD, ElementType.PARAMETER})
public @interface Email { }


  
Three lines of meaning, and each one is load-bearing:


  

    
LineWhat it doesWhat happens if you get it wrong

    
      

        @Qualifier
        Tells CDI this annotation is a label, not just documentation
        Without it the annotation is ignored — and you are back to an ambiguous dependency, with no
          hint that the annotation you wrote is the reason
      

      

        @Retention(RUNTIME)
        Keeps the annotation on the class at run time, which is when CDI reads it
        The default is CLASS, which throws it away before anything can read it. The
          qualifier silently stops working. This is the quietest failure in the whole practical
      

      

        @Target({TYPE, FIELD, PARAMETER})
        Says where the label may legally be written: on a bean class, on a field to inject, or on a
          method parameter
        Too narrow and the compiler stops you (good). Also note there is no @Inherited —
          unlike @RolesAllowed, a qualifier is not passed down to subclasses
      

    
  


  
Now the two beans, and the injection points that pick between them:


  
@Email @ApplicationScoped
public class EmailNotifier implements Notifier { … }

@Sms @ApplicationScoped
public class SmsNotifier implements Notifier { … }

// …and in TransferNotifier:
@Inject @Email Notifier email;
@Inject @Sms   Notifier sms;


  
Read the last two lines as English: "give me the Notifier labelled
    Email". There is no lookup, no string, no cast, and no branch anywhere in the
    application that decides between them. If somebody adds a PushNotifier next month, not one
    existing file changes.


  
An interceptor, and the file it is not in


  
Every transfer, deposit and withdrawal now prints two lines to the server log — one before the method
    runs and one after, with how long it took. Look for the code that does this in
    AccountService and you will not find it. There is one word: @Logged.


  
In plain words
    
An interceptor is code that wraps a method call — it runs before, decides whether to
      let the call proceed, and can run after. In this project it is used for timing and logging; the same
      mechanism is what a framework uses for transactions and security, which is why it is worth
      understanding rather than treating as magic.

    
The binding is the annotation you put on your code (@Logged) that says
      "wrap this". The interceptor is the class that does the wrapping. One binding can be
      applied to a whole class in one word, which is why @Logged sits on the class and not on
      ten methods.

  


  
@Interceptor
@Logged
@Priority(Interceptor.Priority.APPLICATION)
public class BankAuditInterceptor {

    @AroundInvoke
    public Object audit(InvocationContext ctx) throws Exception {
        String method = ctx.getMethod().getName();
        long start = System.nanoTime();
        try {
            return ctx.proceed();               // run the real method
        } finally {
            long ms = (System.nanoTime() - start) / 1_000_000;
            LOG.info(() -> bankName + ": <- " + method + " (" + ms + " ms)");
        }
    }
}


  
    
    
  • @Interceptor — this class is one.
    • 
    
  • @Logged — it is bound to that annotation. A class carrying @Interceptor
      without a binding is never called.
    • 
    
  • @AroundInvoke — this is the method that wraps. ctx.proceed() runs the
      real method, and its return value is the real method's answer. Forget to call
      proceed() and the method never runs — which is exactly how a security
      interceptor refuses a call.
    • 
    
  • The try/finally matters: the "<-" line is printed even when the method throws, which
      is what you want from a timer.
    • 
  


  
🔑

      beans.xml
      
Most material you will find online — and a great deal of it is still current — insists that an
        interceptor must be listed in beans.xml:

      
<interceptors>
    <class>com.globalbank.interceptors.BankAuditInterceptor</class>
</interceptors>

      
That works, and this project's beans.xml is empty anyway — so open it and see. Both
        routes switch the interceptor on:

This project uses @Priority because there is then no file to forget. It is also worth
        knowing what the number means: APPLICATION is 2000, and lower
        numbers run further out. Platform interceptors sit below it, which means by the time this
        interceptor runs, the transaction is already open — so timing a method here includes the
        transaction, which is what you would want it to mean.

Part 6 · The event that waits for the commit


  
When a transfer succeeds, the bank now emails the customer, texts the customer, and writes a line
    saying it did. Look at AccountService.moveMoney() and find where any of that happens.


  
It does not. The method ends with one line:


  
transfers.fire(new TransferCompletedEvent(fromId, toId, amount,
                                          from.getHolder(), to.getHolder()));


  
That is the whole of what the service knows. It does not know that a notifier exists, how many there
    are, what they do, or whether any of them works. It announces, and stops.


  
In plain words
    
An event is an announcement with no addressee. fire() says "this
      happened"; it does not say who should care, and it does not wait to find out. Anything that has
      registered an interest is called by the container, on the same thread, before fire()
      returns.

    
This is the difference between telling two people and publishing a notice. Telling means
      you need their names. Publishing means you need a noticeboard — and adding a third interested party
      later requires no change to the person who put the notice up.

  


  
An observer registers that interest. It is a method with a parameter annotated
    @Observes, and it is never called by your code — the container calls it:


  
public void onTransfer(
        @Observes(during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS) TransferCompletedEvent event) {

    String message = event.amount().toPlainString()
            + " moved from account " + event.fromId() + " (" + event.fromHolder() + ")"
            + " to account " + event.toId() + " (" + event.toHolder() + ")";

    email.send(message);
    sms.send(message);
}


  
🔑
    
during = AFTER_SUCCESS — the most important three words in this
      practical
      
fire() is called inside the transfer's transaction, before the commit. If
        the observer ran at that moment, the bank would tell a customer "£25 has moved" while the database
        still had the option of rolling the whole thing back. The customer would have been told about
        money that then failed to move.

      
during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS tells the container to hold the event
        until the transaction has committed. If the transaction rolls back, the observer is never
        called at all.

      
And the default is not that. The default is IN_PROGRESS — fire immediately, inside
        the transaction. It is the right default for an observer that is doing more work on the
        transaction; it is exactly the wrong one for a notification. An absent during
        is a silent bug, not a neutral choice.

      

        
PhaseThe observer runs…Right for

        
          
IN_PROGRESS (the default)immediately, inside the transactionmore work that belongs to the transaction, and should roll back with it

          
AFTER_SUCCESSafter the commit, only if it committednotifications, printing, anything the outside world can see

          
AFTER_FAILUREafter a rollbackalerting somebody that something failed

          
AFTER_COMPLETIONafter eithercleanup that must happen regardless

        
      

    

  


  
Two more details in that observer are deliberate, and both are worth noticing.


  
The payload is a record, not an entity. An event carrying a live JPA
    Account would hand the observer an object still tied to a persistence context that, by
    the time AFTER_SUCCESS fires, has already been closed. A record holding values — two ids,
    an amount, two names — is immutable, and freezing the values is the honest thing to do. The
    announcement describes what happened, not the current state of the database.


  
Both notifiers are injected by qualifier — the same @Email and
    @Sms from Part 5. So this one class is where the two ideas meet: an event says
    when to notify, and qualifiers say which notifiers to use.

Part 7 · Ship it: what an EAR would change (Session 14)


  
🚨
    
Read this before the rest of the part
      
Everything up to here is a war, and it runs. This part is reference material, not a
        build instruction. The multi-module EAR below is shown in full so that you can see its
        shape — and build it yourself if you want to — but the thing this practical's folder guarantees to
        produce is globalbank-web.war.

      
That is a deliberate decision and not a gap. An EAR is four Maven projects with a parent, and
        every extra module is another place for a beginner's build to fail before they have seen the
        application work even once. Get the war running first. That is now done. So:
        what is an EAR for?

    

  


  
A war bundles web components. An ejb-jar bundles enterprise beans. An
    EAR bundles them together, plus any plain libraries they share. You move to one when you want the
    beans and the pages to be separately deployable, or when more than one module needs the same library
    and you are tired of copying it into each.


  
The layout

  
globalbank-ear/                 <packaging>pom</packaging>   ← the parent
├── pom.xml                     <modules> lists the three below
├── globalbank-ejb/             <packaging>ejb</packaging>   ← the beans move here
│   └── src/main/java/com/globalbank/{ejb,entity,messaging,dao,notify,events,…}
├── globalbank-web/             <packaging>war</packaging>   ← the pages stay here
│   └── src/main/{java/com/globalbank/{web,security},webapp}
└── globalbank-ear/             <packaging>ear</packaging>   ← the package itself
    └── src/main/application/META-INF/application.xml


  
Note how little moves. The entities, the service beans, the queue, the notifiers, the
    events and the interceptors all belong to globalbank-ejb. The page, its backing bean, the
    servlet and the security package belong to globalbank-web. Not one line of
    Java changes — this is a question about where files live, and about what Maven produces from them.


  
🚨
    
persistence.xml moves with the entities
      
A persistence unit belongs to the module that owns the entities, not to the war. Leave the file
        behind and you get "no persistence unit named globalbankPU" at deploy time, which reads like
        a typo and is really a packaging mistake.

      
This is the single most common thing to get wrong when splitting a war in two.

    

  


  
The three poms


  
The parent — this is the file that makes four projects build as one. It builds
    nothing itself; it declares the modules and the versions:


  
<packaging>pom</packaging>          <!-- pom, not war: this builds nothing itself -->

<modules>
  <module>globalbank-ejb</module>
  <module>globalbank-web</module>
  <module>globalbank-ear</module>
</modules>

<dependencyManagement>          <!-- declare each version ONCE -->
  <dependencies>
    <dependency>
      <groupId>jakarta.platform</groupId>
      <artifactId>jakarta.jakartaee-api</artifactId>
      <version>10.0.0</version>
      <scope>provided</scope>
    </dependency>
  </dependencies>
</dependencyManagement>


  
<dependencyManagement> is the section that stops you repeating a version number in
    four places. It declares a version once; each module then lists the dependency with no
    <version> at all.


  
The EJB module — one line matters:


  
<artifactId>globalbank-ejb</artifactId>
<packaging>ejb</packaging>      <!-- builds a jar the container treats as a bean archive -->


  
The web module — and this is the line everybody misses:


  
<dependency>
  <groupId>com.globalbank</groupId>
  <artifactId>globalbank-ejb</artifactId>
  <version>1.0.0</version>
  <type>ejb</type>              <!-- `ejb`, not `jar` — an EJB module's type -->
  <scope>provided</scope>       <!-- the EAR supplies it; do not nest a copy in the war -->
</dependency>


  
The war does not get the beans from the classpath. Without this dependency the page's Java compiles
    against nothing and the classes are not visible at run time either. And provided matters
    for a reason worth understanding: a second copy of those classes inside
    WEB-INF/lib is how you end up with two different Account classes and a
    ClassCastException that makes no sense.


  
The EAR module — this is where <library-directory> comes from:


  
<artifactId>globalbank-ear</artifactId>
<packaging>ear</packaging>

<plugin>
  <artifactId>maven-ear-plugin</artifactId>
  <configuration>
    <version>10</version>                                <!-- Jakarta EE 10 -->
    <defaultLibBundleDir>lib</defaultLibBundleDir>       <!-- <library-directory> -->
    <modules>
      <ejbModule>
        <artifactId>globalbank-ejb</artifactId>
        <bundleFileName>globalbank-ejb.jar</bundleFileName>
      </ejbModule>
      <webModule>
        <artifactId>globalbank-web</artifactId>
        <contextRoot>/globalbank</contextRoot>           <!-- the URL changes too -->
      </webModule>
    </modules>
  </configuration>
</plugin>


  
<defaultLibBundleDir>lib</defaultLibBundleDir> is the
    <library-directory> Session 14 talks about: shared jars go in the EAR's
    lib/ folder, which is on the classpath of every module, instead of being duplicated inside
    the war.

security/LoginBean.java — the piece Session 13 does not show you, and
      the reason a login can silently do nothing.

    
@Named("login")
@RequestScoped
public class LoginBean {

    @Inject
    private SecurityContext securityContext;

    private String username;
    private String password;
    private String message;

    public String login() {
        if (username == null || username.isBlank() || password == null || password.isEmpty()) {
            message = "Enter your user name and password.";
            return null;
        }

        FacesContext faces = FacesContext.getCurrentInstance();
        ExternalContext external = faces.getExternalContext();
        HttpServletRequest request = (HttpServletRequest) external.getRequest();
        HttpServletResponse response = (HttpServletResponse) external.getResponse();

        Credential credential =
                new UsernamePasswordCredential(username, new Password(password));

        AuthenticationParameters params =
                AuthenticationParameters.withParams().credential(credential);

        AuthenticationStatus status =
                securityContext.authenticate(request, response, params);

        password = null;                 // out of the heap as soon as it is no longer needed

        return switch (status) {
            case SUCCESS -> "/accounts.xhtml?faces-redirect=true";
            case SEND_CONTINUE -> null;  // the mechanism is mid-redirect; say nothing
            case SEND_FAILURE -> {       // one message for "wrong password" and "no such user"
                message = "That user name and password combination was not recognised.";
                yield null;
            }
            case NOT_DONE -> {           // a developer problem, so log it as one
                LOG.warning("Authentication was not attempted - check the mechanism definition.");
                message = "Sign-in could not be completed. See the server log.";
                yield null;
            }
            default -> { message = "Sign-in could not be completed."; yield null; }
        };
    }

    public String logout() {
        FacesContext.getCurrentInstance().getExternalContext().invalidateSession();
        return "/login.xhtml?faces-redirect=true";
    }
}

    
Read the switch as a list of the four things the container can say back. And read the
      password = null line as the habit it is: a password that is no longer needed should not
      be sitting in memory waiting for a heap dump.

@Singleton
@Startup
public class UserInitializer {

    @PersistenceContext private EntityManager em;
    @Inject private Pbkdf2PasswordHash hasher;

    @PostConstruct
    public void seed() {
        hasher.initialize(new java.util.HashMap<>());   // state the defaults, do not assume them

        Long existing = em.createQuery("SELECT COUNT(u) FROM BankUser u", Long.class)
                          .getSingleResult();
        if (existing > 0) return;

        addUser("ada@globalbank.test",     "secret",  "CUSTOMER");
        addUser("manager@globalbank.test", "manager", "BANK_MANAGER");
    }

    private void addUser(String username, String password, String groupName) {
        String hash = hasher.generate(password.toCharArray());
        em.persist(new BankUser(username, hash, groupName));
    }
}

    
Generate the hash; never assemble it. That four-part string has an iteration
      count, Base64 encoding and padding, and a salt that must be fresh every time — hand-writing one is
      how you get a password that cannot be verified. And generate() takes a
      char[] rather than a String on purpose: a String sits in
      memory until the garbage collector happens to reclaim it, and a char[] can be
      overwritten the moment you are done.


    
ejb/AccountService.java — the boundary, in annotations.

    
@Stateless
@Logged
@DeclareRoles({"CUSTOMER", "BANK_MANAGER"})
public class AccountService implements AccountServiceRemote {

    @Inject private Event<TransferCompletedEvent> transfers;

    @RolesAllowed({"CUSTOMER", "BANK_MANAGER"})
    public String transfer(int fromId, int toId, BigDecimal amount)
            throws InsufficientFundsException {
        return moveMoney(fromId, toId, amount);
    }

    /** The MDB has no caller, so there is no identity for a role check to test. */
    @PermitAll
    public String transferOrRefuse(int fromId, int toId, BigDecimal amount) {
        try {
            return moveMoney(fromId, toId, amount);
        } catch (InsufficientFundsException | IllegalArgumentException e) {
            return "REFUSED: " + e.getMessage();
        }
    }

    /** Private, so neither entry point can accidentally inherit the other's rules. */
    private String moveMoney(int fromId, int toId, BigDecimal amount)
            throws InsufficientFundsException {
        // …balance check, two setBalance calls, then:
        transfers.fire(new TransferCompletedEvent(fromId, toId, amount,
                                                  from.getHolder(), to.getHolder()));
        return "Transferred " + amount.toPlainString() + " …";
    }
}

    
Practical 2 had one transfer method and the security question never came up. The
      refactor into moveMoney is what makes it possible to say, precisely, which door the
      rule applies to — and to keep it true as the class grows.


    
config/BankConfiguration.java — both producer shapes, and the rule
      about proxies.

    
@ApplicationScoped
public class BankConfiguration {

    /** A @Produces FIELD: for a value that is simply known. */
    @Produces @BankName
    String bankName = "GlobalBank";

    /** A @Produces METHOD: for a value that has to be worked out. */
    @Produces @AuditDepth
    int auditDepth() {
        String raw = System.getProperty("globalbank.auditDepth", "25");
        try {
            int value = Integer.parseInt(raw.trim());
            return value > 0 ? value : 25;
        } catch (NumberFormatException e) {
            return 25;
        }
    }
}

    
Neither carries a scope, and they must not. A normal scope means a proxy, a proxy
      means subclassing, and String is final while int is a
      primitive. Adding @ApplicationScoped to either of these is a definition error that
      refuses the deployment.


    
events/TransferNotifier.java — the two ideas meeting.

    
@ApplicationScoped
public class TransferNotifier {

    @Inject @Email private Notifier email;      // the qualifier says WHICH notifier
    @Inject @Sms   private Notifier sms;

    public void onTransfer(
            @Observes(during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS)
            TransferCompletedEvent event) {     // …and the phase says WHEN

        String message = event.amount().toPlainString()
                + " moved from account " + event.fromId() + " (" + event.fromHolder() + ")"
                + " to account " + event.toId() + " (" + event.toHolder() + ")";

        email.send(message);
        sms.send(message);
    }
}

    
Eight lines. One class that owns when to tell the customer, two qualifiers that decide
      how, and an AccountService that knows about none of it.


  


  
Debugging: the five things that actually go wrong

  

    
What you are looking atWhere to look first

    
      

        The login page redisplays and nothing happens
        LoginBean.login() is not being called. With a custom form mechanism,
          nothing authenticates unless your code asks — check the action on the button.
      

      

        A customer gets an error page where the audit log should be
        getAuditLines() was reached for a customer. JSF evaluates expressions inside
          rendered="false", so the guard in the bean is the one that matters.
      

      

        Ambiguous dependencies for type Notifier
        A @Email or @Sms is missing from an injection point. The error names
          both candidates — that is the whole diagnosis.
      

      

        Deployment refused, and the message mentions proxy
        A normal scope on a @Produces of String or int. Remove
          the scope; they are final or primitive.
      

      

        The customer is told about a transfer that later failed
        The observer has no during, so it defaults to IN_PROGRESS and runs
          inside the transaction. Add
          during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS.

One-Line Summary

  

    
Two annotations on an empty class describe the vault
      (@CustomFormAuthenticationMechanismDefinition,
      @DatabaseIdentityStoreDefinition) and one bean is the handle
      (LoginBean, calling SecurityContext.authenticate — and doing nothing
      at all if you leave it out). web.xml decides which URLs need a signed-in visitor,
      @RolesAllowed decides which methods need which role, and a method with neither is
      open to everyone. @Produces puts a configuration value in one place,
      @Qualifier picks between two beans without an if,
      @Priority switches an interceptor on with no XML, and
      @Observes(during = AFTER_SUCCESS) makes sure the customer is told about a transfer
      only once the database has agreed it happened. Then, if you want it, four Maven projects and a
      <library-directory> turn all of it into an EAR — for the price of a new URL and a
      new JNDI name.

Common mistakes to avoid

  

    
❌ The mistake✅ The fix

    
      

        Configuring a custom form mechanism and expecting the container to perform the login
        Write the bean that calls SecurityContext.authenticate(request, response, params).
          With a custom mechanism, nothing happens unless your code asks — and it fails silently.
      

      

        Assuming a method with no security annotation is protected
        It is unchecked. Annotate every public method on a secured bean, including the ones
          that are meant to be open — @PermitAll is documentation as much as it is policy.
      

      

        Relying on rendered="false" to protect something
        It draws nothing and protects nothing — and JSF still evaluates the expressions inside it. The
          rule goes on the bean method; the page attribute is presentation.
      

      

        Trusting a friendly check in a servlet as the only check
        Keep it for the readable message, but let the container be the enforcement. A servlet check
          only covers the routes through that servlet.
      

      

        Putting a normal scope on a producer of String or int
        Leave it @Dependent. A normal scope means a proxy, and a proxy means subclassing
          something that cannot be subclassed — a definition error that stops the deployment.
      

      

        Omitting @Retention(RUNTIME) on a qualifier
        Add it. The default CLASS retention discards the annotation before CDI can read
          it, and the qualifier silently stops working with nothing in the log.
      

      

        Writing @Observes without during for a notification
        Use during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS. The default is
          IN_PROGRESS, which tells the customer about money that may still be rolled back.
      

      

        Forgetting ctx.proceed() in an @AroundInvoke method
        Call it. proceed() is the method call — forgetting it silently swallows
          the operation. In a security interceptor that is deliberate; anywhere else it is a bug.
      

      

        Hand-writing a PBKDF2 hash string
        Generate it: Pbkdf2PasswordHash.generate(char[]). The iteration count, salt and
          Base64 padding are not things to reproduce by hand.
      

      

        Giving "no such user" and "wrong password" different messages
        One message for both. The distinction tells an attacker which user names are real, and that is
          half of what they need.
      

      

        Renaming the war when you build the next stage
        Keep the name. A renamed artefact silently breaks every URL and the JNDI name you have already
          written down — which is precisely how a real upgrade goes wrong.

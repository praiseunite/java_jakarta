Part 1 · All of it, or none of it: transactions

1.1 The transfer that half happened

Your program moves $1,000 from Alice to Bob. That looks like one action. It is not — underneath, it is two database steps:

  1. Take $1,000 out of Alice's account. (This is called a debit.)
  2. Put $1,000 into Bob's account. (This is called a credit.)

Now imagine the power fails after step 1 and before step 2. Alice is $1,000 poorer. Bob has nothing. The money has simply vanished — and no amount of careful Java prevents a power cut.

Every serious business system has this same shape of problem:

  • An order is placed, but the stock is never reduced.
  • A seat is booked, but the payment never completes.
  • A student enrols, but the class list is never updated.

1.2 The fix: a transaction

A transaction is a group of database steps that the system treats as one single step. Either the whole group happens, or none of it happens.

Two new words come with it, and both describe how the group ends:

WordWhat it means
Commit“Everything worked — make all of these changes permanent.”
Rollback“Something failed — undo everything this group did, as if it had never started.”

So the transfer becomes one transaction: the debit and the credit together. If step 2 fails, the system rolls back step 1, and Alice's money is back where it started. That is the whole idea.

In plain words

A transaction is a promise that says: all of these changes, or none of them.

A commit is the moment the changes become real. A rollback is the moment they are quietly undone. There is no in-between state where the customer has been charged but nothing was delivered.

🏠
Analogy Buying a house. You do not hand over the keys on Monday and simply hope the money turns up on Tuesday. Buyer and seller meet at the notary's table: the money moves and the keys change hands in the same moment. If either half falls through, the whole deal is called off and the keys go back in the seller's pocket.

1.3 The four promises: ACID

Transactions are not new — databases have offered them for decades. The guarantees they make are summed up by four letters, ACID. One letter, one promise.

Here they all are, with the banking example that matters:

PromiseWhat it guaranteesBanking example
A — Atomicity “All or nothing.” The debit and the credit both commit, or both roll back. There is no such thing as half a transfer.
C — Consistency “Every rule still holds before and after.” An account may not fall below its overdraft limit of −$500. If a transaction would break that rule, it aborts.
I — Isolation “Running transactions cannot see each other.” While Alice's transfer is still in progress, Bob cannot see the half-deposited balance. He sees the balance as it was before, until the commit.
D — Durability “A committed change survives a crash.” Once committed and acknowledged, pulling the server's power plug will never erase the transaction record.
In plain words

Atomicity is the all-or-nothing promise. Consistency says every rule still holds when the dust settles. Isolation says two transactions running at once cannot peek at each other's unfinished work. Durability says once it is committed, it is permanent — even if the power dies one second later.

💡
The one thing to notice These four promises are not features you write. They are guarantees the database and the transaction manager give you — but only if you group your steps into one transaction. Forget that, and you can have a fast, tidy-looking program that still loses money.

1.4 Who draws the line? CMT versus BMT

A transaction has a boundary: a clear start and a clear end. Deciding where that boundary sits is called demarcation — a long word for “where does the transaction begin, and where does it finish?”

There are two ways to decide, and in real life you will only ever use one.

Container-Managed Transactions (CMT)Bean-Managed Transactions (BMT)
Who controls it Declarative. You put a label on the method; the container opens, commits and rolls back for you. Programmatic. You call userTx.begin() and userTx.commit() yourself.
How hard is it Very simple. No boilerplate code at all. Hard and easy to get wrong. Miss one rollback inside an exception handler and you quietly corrupt someone's data.
Which is the default Yes. More than 95% of enterprise beans use CMT. No. Only used when you need a finer-grained checkpoint in the middle of a method.
🧾
Analogy With CMT you hire an accountant: you mark the folder, and a professional keeps the books correct. With BMT you keep the books yourself. One of those scales to a whole company; the other works beautifully right up until the day you miss a line.
In plain words

CMT means “I write a label on the method and the container draws the boundary.” That is the entire job. BMT means “I write the start and the end myself, by hand.”

Start with CMT. In this course you will almost never need BMT.

1.6 The six transaction attributes: joining a phone call

  
You have chosen CMT, so the container draws the transaction boundary. But which
    boundary? Every method can be called in one of two situations:

  
    
    
  • the caller is already inside a transaction, or
    • 
    
  • the caller is not in any transaction at all.
    • 
  

  
The transaction attribute is the label you put on a method to say how it behaves
    in each of those two cases. There are six of them, and they look scarier than they are — because
    each one is just an answer to a single question.


  
Think of it as a phone call

  
You are the specialist at a desk. Someone calls you. Before you start talking, you must decide
    what to do about the call that may already be in progress. Each attribute is one possible
    answer:


  

    
AttributeIn one sentence

    
      

        REQUIRED (default)
        “Is a call already happening? Then I join it. If not, I start a new one.” This is the default, and it is what you want almost every time.
      

      

        REQUIRES_NEW
        “I do not care what else is happening. I open a fresh, separate call of my own — and my call still counts even if the other one collapses.”
      

      

        SUPPORTS
        “If a call is happening, I join it. If not, I just chat — no call needed.”
      

      

        NOT_SUPPORTED
        “Put the current call on hold. I will do my work with no call at all.”
      

      

        MANDATORY
        “I will only work if a call is already in progress. If nobody started one, I refuse — that is an error.”
      

      

        NEVER
        “I will not work while a call is in progress. If one is happening, I refuse.”
      

    
  


  
In plain words
    
REQUIRED and REQUIRES_NEW are the two you will actually use.
      REQUIRED says “join whatever is going on, or start something if nothing is.”
      REQUIRES_NEW says “my work is so important that it must be recorded separately,
      even if everything around me fails.”

    
SUPPORTS and NOT_SUPPORTED are for read-only work that does not care.
      MANDATORY and NEVER exist to catch mistakes: they refuse to run in the
      wrong situation rather than doing something surprising.

  


  
Why REQUIRES_NEW matters: the audit log

  
An audit log records who did what. Suppose the transfer fails and rolls back — do you want the
    attempt to vanish from the log too? No. The log is supposed to say “Alice tried to move
    $1,000 and it failed.”

  
So the logging method is marked REQUIRES_NEW. Its work runs in its own separate
    transaction and commits on its own, whatever happens to the transfer around it.


  
AuditLogBean.java — the audit method marked REQUIRES_NEW

  
package com.globalbank.ejb;

import jakarta.ejb.Stateless;
import jakarta.ejb.TransactionAttribute;
import jakarta.ejb.TransactionAttributeType;

@Stateless
public class AuditLogBean {

    @TransactionAttribute(TransactionAttributeType.REQUIRES_NEW)
    public void recordAttempt(String user, double amount) {
        // Runs in its own fresh transaction: this line is committed
        // even if the transfer around it rolls back.
        auditStore.append(user + " attempted to move $" + amount);
    }
}


  
That annotation is the whole change: the container suspends the caller's transaction and lets
    this method commit on its own.


  

  
⚠️
    
The trap in these two words
      MANDATORY and NEVER are not a security setting, and they do not
      mean “strict” and “never used”. MANDATORY means “I must be called from inside a
      transaction, or I refuse to run.” NEVER means “I must never run inside a
      transaction.” Choosing them because of how the word sounds is a classic beginner mistake.

  


  
💡
    
If you remember only one thing from Part 1
      REQUIRED is the default for a reason. Use it everywhere, and reach for
      REQUIRES_NEW only when a piece of work genuinely must survive a caller's failure —
      audit logs, notifications, counters.

Part 2 · Two users, one row: stopping lost updates


  
2.1 The lost update

  
Transactions keep you safe when something fails. This part is about a different danger:
    when two things succeed, but at the same moment.

  
An account holds $1,000. Two tellers work at the same instant:

  
    
    
  • Teller 1 reads the balance: $1,000. Plans to withdraw $800 → new balance $200.
    • 
    
  • Teller 2 reads the balance: $1,000. Plans to withdraw $600 → new balance $400.
    • 
  

  
Both tellers read the same starting number. Teller 1 saves $200. Teller 2 saves $400, overwriting
    Teller 1's change. The bank has paid out $1,400 from a $1,000 account, and the final balance is
    wrong. One of the two updates was silently lost. This is called a
    lost update.


  
📋
    
Analogy
      A shared shopping whiteboard. Two people look at it at the same moment: “milk, eggs.” Both walk
      away, both decide to add one item, and both write out the whole list again. Whoever
      writes last wins — and the other person's item has vanished from the board.

  


  
2.2 Two ways to stop it

  
There are exactly two strategies, and their names describe the personality of the person using
    them. Optimistic means “I will assume nothing goes wrong.”
    Pessimistic means “I will assume it does.”


  
Optimistic locking takes no lock at all when you read. Instead the row carries a
    little counter — a version number. Every time the row is saved, the counter goes up by one. When
    your save arrives, the system checks the counter: is it still the version I read? If
    somebody else bumped it first, your save is refused, and you re-read the row and try again.


  
Pessimistic locking does the opposite. The row is locked the moment you read it.
    Everybody else who wants that row waits in a queue until you release the lock.


  

    
StrategyHow it worksHow you write itBest for

    
      

        Optimistic
        No lock on read. A version counter is checked at commit time, and the save fails if another thread bumped the version first.
        @Version private int version; on the JPA entity
        Read-heavy systems — high scalability, conflicts are rare.
      

      

        Pessimistic
        The database row is locked immediately on read. Later threads queue up until the lock is freed.
        em.find(..., LockModeType.PESSIMISTIC_WRITE)
        High-conflict financial writes, where collisions are expected.
      

    
  


  
Two of those words need a translation. A JPA entity is a Java class mapped to a
    database table, and em is the EntityManager — the object that talks to the
    database for you. The JPA bonus page tells that story
    properly; for now, read both as “the database side of things”.


  
🔑
    
Analogy — the petrol-station key
      To use the petrol station's toilet you must take the single key on its big wooden stick. While you
      have it, everyone else waits. That is pessimistic locking: take the key, hold up
      everyone else, hand it back when you are done.

  


  
🕐
    
Analogy — the date stamp on a form
      You print a form, fill it in at your desk, and bring it back. Nobody blocked you while you worked.
      But before filing it, the clerk checks the date: “somebody filed a newer version while you were
      away — your copy is stale, do it again.” That is optimistic locking: no waiting
      at all, but the clash is caught at the very end.

  


  
In plain words
    
Optimistic locking says: let everybody work, and refuse the save if the data changed
      underneath them. It is fast, and it works because clashes are rare.

    
Pessimistic locking says: only one person may even look at this row. It is safe under
      heavy conflict, but everybody else stands in a queue.

  


  
2.3 When one object is shared by everyone

  
Recall Session 1: a @Singleton bean is one single instance, shared by
    the whole application. That is the lost-update situation again — but happening in memory instead of
    in a database row.

  
By default the container handles it for you. This is called Container-Managed
    Concurrency (CMC): you say who may come in, using @Lock, and the container
    makes it true.


  

    
LockWhat it allowsUse it for

    
      
@Lock(LockType.READ)Many threads at the same time.Methods that only read — nothing can be corrupted.

      
@Lock(LockType.WRITE)Exactly one thread. Everyone else queues.Methods that change the shared data.

    
  


  
There is a second, quieter question: if a thread has to wait for the lock, how long should
    it wait? That is what @AccessTimeout answers — give up after this long, rather than
    hanging forever.


  
ApplicationCacheBean.java — controlled concurrency

  
package com.globalbank.ejb;

import jakarta.ejb.Singleton;
import jakarta.ejb.ConcurrencyManagement;
import jakarta.ejb.ConcurrencyManagementType;
import jakarta.ejb.Lock;
import jakarta.ejb.LockType;
import jakarta.ejb.AccessTimeout;
import java.util.concurrent.TimeUnit;

@Singleton
@ConcurrencyManagement(ConcurrencyManagementType.CONTAINER)
public class ApplicationCacheBean {

    private String exchangeRates;

    // Multiple threads can read concurrently without blocking each other
    @Lock(LockType.READ)
    public String getRates() {
        return this.exchangeRates;
    }

    // Only ONE thread can write at a time. Other callers will wait up to 3 seconds
    @Lock(LockType.WRITE)
    @AccessTimeout(value = 3, unit = TimeUnit.SECONDS)
    public void updateRates(String newRates) {
        this.exchangeRates = newRates;
    }
}


  
🔬
    
The one thing to notice
      @ConcurrencyManagement(ConcurrencyManagementType.CONTAINER) means “container, you
      decide who enters — I will just label each method.” After that, the entire safety of the shared
      exchangeRates field comes down to one word per method: READ for
      looking, WRITE for changing.

Part 3 · Don't make the user wait: events and background work


  
3.1 Ten thousand clicks at once

  
Ten thousand customers press “Pay now” in the same second.

  
If every request does the whole job before replying — charge the card, update the database, send
    the receipt email, write the audit line — then every one of those ten thousand browsers sits
    waiting. The page looks frozen. The server buckles.

  
The fix is to stop thinking in terms of calls and start thinking in terms of
    events: “this happened — somebody deal with it.” The user's page can reply
    immediately while the real work goes on elsewhere.


  
3.2 Two ways to hand off work

  
Jakarta EE gives you two quite different tools for this. Choosing between them is really a question
    of how far away the listener is.


  

    
MechanismReachHow it decouples

    
      

        Message-driven bean (MDB)
        Distributed — across servers, via Jakarta Messaging (JMS — the standard way Java programs
          send messages to each other).
        A publisher drops a message on a Queue or a Topic; an MDB picks it up in the background, possibly on a completely different machine.
      

      

        CDI events (@Observes)
        In-process — inside one running application.
        A producer calls event.fire(...); the observer methods react immediately. No queue, no separate service.
      

    
  


  
In plain words
    
Both of them let one part of your program say “something happened” without knowing — or
      caring — who is listening.

    
The MDB version ships the message across the network and answers the user instantly. The CDI
      version shouts inside the same building, and the listeners react at once.

  


  
3.3 The queue: a message-driven bean

  
A message-driven bean sits in the background and listens. When a message arrives,
    the container wakes the bean, it does its work, and it goes back to listening. The sender never
    waits for an answer — that is what asynchronous means.


  
🎫
    
Analogy
      A busy takeaway counter. You pay, get a numbered ticket, and sit down — you are not standing at
      the counter watching your food being cooked. The kitchen works through the tickets in order. Your
      ticket is a message on a queue; the kitchen is the MDB.

  


  
You met MDBs properly in Session 1 & 2, and they get a whole session of their own later. For
    this page the point is simply this: a queue is the tool you reach for when the
    listener is somewhere else, or when the work must survive even if this server restarts.


  
A topic is the close cousin of a queue, and the difference is worth one sentence:
    a queue delivers each message to one consumer, while a topic delivers a copy to
    every subscriber. Reach for a topic when several systems all need to hear the same
    announcement.


  
3.4 The announcement: CDI events

  
Sometimes the listener is not far away at all — it is another class in the same
    application. For that, CDI gives you events. There are three small pieces:

  
    
    
  1. an event object — a plain class carrying the details of what happened;
    2. 
    
  2. a producer — the code that fires the event, without knowing who listens;
    3. 
    
  3. an observer — a method marked @Observes, which runs when the event
      is fired.
    4. 
  


  
📢
    
Analogy
      A fire alarm in an office. The person who pressed it has no idea who will respond. The floor
      warden heads for the stairs, the kitchen switches off the ovens, security opens the doors. One
      announcement, several independent reactions — and none of them needed anybody's phone number.

  


  
PaymentCompletedEvent.java — the event payload

  
package com.globalbank.events;

// A plain bag of details — nothing more.
public class PaymentCompletedEvent {
    public final int accountId;
    public final double amount;
    public PaymentCompletedEvent(int id, double amt) { this.accountId = id; this.amount = amt; }
}


  
PaymentBean.java — the producer

  
package com.globalbank.events;

import jakarta.ejb.Stateless;
import jakarta.enterprise.event.Event;
import jakarta.inject.Inject;

@Stateless
public class PaymentBean {

    @Inject
    private Event<PaymentCompletedEvent> paymentEvents;

    public void processPayment(int id, double amt) {
        // complete the payment ...
        paymentEvents.fire(new PaymentCompletedEvent(id, amt));
    }
}


  
AuditAndSMSNotificationBean.java — the observer

  
package com.globalbank.events;

import jakarta.ejb.Stateless;
import jakarta.enterprise.event.Observes;

@Stateless
public class AuditAndSMSNotificationBean {
    public void onPaymentDone(@Observes PaymentCompletedEvent event) {
        System.out.println("Triggered SMS receipt for Account: " + event.accountId);
    }
}


  
🔬
    
The one thing to notice
      PaymentBean never mentions AuditAndSMSNotificationBean. No field, no
      import, no reference of any kind. It fires an event and walks away. You could add a third listener
      tomorrow — fraud detection, a loyalty-points counter — and never touch
      PaymentBean at all.

  


  
⚠️
    
Careful: events fired inside a transaction
      A CDI observer runs during the producer's call by default, so it can still be part of the
      same transaction — which is usually what you want, but not always. If you want the reaction to
      happen only after the transaction commits, say so explicitly with
      @Observes(during = TransactionPhase.AFTER_SUCCESS). Do not assume it is automatic.

Part 4 · Who is allowed in: security with a label


  
4.1 The problem

  
Bank branch opening hours should be readable by anyone. A balance lookup should be available to a
    teller or a manager. Authorising a credit line should be for managers only. And an old internal
    debug method should be usable by nobody.

  
You could write an if statement at the top of every single method. Most beginners do.
    It works, right up to the day somebody adds a new method and forgets the if.

  
Jakarta EE lets you do it instead with one label above the method.


  
4.2 Security happens in three places, not one

  
Before the labels, look at the whole picture. Good security is not one wall — it is several layers,
    so that a hole in one does not become a hole in the system.


  

  

    
LayerWhat it protectsEveryday comparison

    
      

        Application layer
        Who may do what. Roles and permissions on your own methods — declared or written by hand.
        The guest list at the door.
      

      

        Transport layer (TLS)
        Who can read what you send. HTTPS encrypts the traffic between the browser and the server.
        The letter sealed in a tamper-proof envelope.
      

      

        Message layer (WS-Security)
        Trust between systems. Signing and encrypting the message itself, so it stays protected after it is unpacked.
        The signature inside the letter, which still proves who wrote it after the envelope is opened.
      

    
  


  
This session covers the application layer — the part you write yourself. Transport
    is a server setting, and message-layer security belongs to web services.


  
4.3 Declarative security: a label above the method

  
“Declarative” means you declare the rule instead of coding it. There are three annotations
    to know, and they read almost like English.


  

    
AnnotationWho may call this method

    
      
@PermitAllAnyone at all — even a user who never logged in.

      
@RolesAllowed({"TELLER", "MANAGER"})Only users in the listed roles. You can give one role or several.

      
@DenyAllNobody. The method cannot be called through its business interface at all.

    
  


  
🚪
    
Analogy
      A doorman with a guest list. You do not ask the DJ to check IDs, and you do not post your own
      guard. You write the list, and the doorman enforces it on every single person who walks up.

  


  
SecureBankService.java — declarative role access

  
import jakarta.annotation.security.RolesAllowed;
import jakarta.annotation.security.PermitAll;
import jakarta.annotation.security.DenyAll;
import jakarta.ejb.Stateless;

@Stateless
public class SecureBankService {

    @PermitAll
    public String getBankBranchHours() {
        return "9:00 AM - 5:00 PM";
    }

    @RolesAllowed({"TELLER", "MANAGER"})
    public double checkBalance(int accountId) {
        return 5400.25;
    }

    @RolesAllowed("MANAGER")
    public void authorizeCreditLine(int customerId, double limit) {
        // Only managers can authorize loans
    }

    @DenyAll
    public void deprecatedInternalDebugDump() {
        // Blocked for all callers
    }
}


  
🔬
    
The one thing to notice
      There is not one if in that class. @DenyAll does not close the whole
      class — it closes exactly that one method. And @RolesAllowed accepts either a single
      role or an array: @RolesAllowed("MANAGER") and
      @RolesAllowed({"MANAGER"}) mean the same thing.

  


  
4.4 Programmatic security: when the rule depends on the data

  
Labels are perfect for rules that never change: “managers only, always.” But some rules depend on
    the actual value being passed in. For example: “anyone may transfer, but a transfer above
    $10,000 also requires a manager.” No annotation can express that — it needs an
    if.

  
So instead you read the caller's identity at runtime. The container hands it to you through
    EJBContext.


  

    
CallWhat it tells you

    
      

        context.getCallerPrincipal()
        Who is calling — the logged-in user, as a Principal object. Calling .getName() on it gives the username.
      

      

        context.isCallerInRole("MANAGER")
        Whether that user holds a particular role. Returns true or false.
      

    
  


  
TransferService.java — programmatic role check

  
@Stateless
public class TransferService {

    @Resource
    private EJBContext context;

    public void transfer(int from, int to, double amount) {
        java.security.Principal caller = context.getCallerPrincipal();

        if (amount > 10000.00 && !context.isCallerInRole("MANAGER")) {
            throw new SecurityException("Transfers over $10,000 require MANAGER credentials!");
        }
        System.out.println("Audit: Transfer executed by " + caller.getName());
    }
}


  
🔬
    
The one thing to notice
      @Resource asks the container to hand you an EJBContext — you never write
      new for it, exactly like @EJB in Session 1. And note the difference in
      kind: the annotation in section 4.3 protects the whole method, always; this code protects
      one branch, depending on the amount.

    📋 The six transaction attributes, in full
    
There are two columns that matter: what happens when the caller is already inside a
      transaction, and what happens when the caller is not. Read it alongside the phone-call table in
      section 1.6 — this is the same information, spelled out formally.

    

      
AttributeCaller has an active transactionCaller has no transactionTypical use case

      
        

          REQUIRED (default)
          Joins the caller's transaction
          Creates a brand new transaction
          Standard financial business methods (transfer, deposit)
        

        

          REQUIRES_NEW
          Suspends the caller; creates a new transaction
          Creates a brand new transaction
          Audit logging (must commit even if the main transfer rolls back!)
        

        

          MANDATORY
          Joins the caller's transaction
          🚨 Throws an EJB transaction-required exception (EJBTransactionRequiredException)
          Internal helper methods that must not be run standalone
        

        

          NOT_SUPPORTED
          Suspends the caller; runs non-transactionally
          Runs non-transactionally
          Heavy read-only catalog searches or image streaming
        

        

          SUPPORTS
          Joins the caller's transaction
          Runs non-transactionally
          Flexible read utilities (e.g. getting the current exchange rates)
        

        

          NEVER
          🚨 Throws EJBException (wrapped in RemoteException for remote clients)
          Runs non-transactionally
          Long-running batch report generators that must never hold locks
        

      
    

    
The one-sentence summary: REQUIRED and REQUIRES_NEW are
      the two you will actually write; SUPPORTS and NOT_SUPPORTED are for work
      that does not care; MANDATORY and NEVER refuse to run in the wrong
      situation.

  


  

    🔒 Isolation levels — how much can two transactions see?
    
Isolation was the “I” in ACID. Databases let you trade strictness against speed, using four named
      levels. First, the three problems they exist to prevent:

    

      
SymptomWhat happens

      
        
Dirty readYou read a value another transaction has changed but not yet committed — and then it rolls back. You acted on a number that never existed.

        
Non-repeatable readYou read the same row twice inside one transaction and get two different values, because someone else committed a change in between.

        
Phantom readYou run the same query twice and a new row appears the second time, because someone else inserted one.

      
    

    

      
Isolation levelDirty readNon-repeatable readPhantom read

      
        
READ_UNCOMMITTEDPossiblePossiblePossible

        
READ_COMMITTED (the usual default)PreventedPossiblePossible

        
REPEATABLE_READPreventedPreventedPossible

        
SERIALIZABLEPreventedPreventedPrevented

      
    

    
Stricter means slower, because more work is being locked or retried. In Jakarta EE
      you do not normally set this per method with an annotation — it is a property of the database or
      the data source, and most applications simply run at the database's default.

  


  

    ↩️ What the container rolls back, and what it does not
    
You saw a rollback in section 1.5. The rule for when it happens is not “on any exception” —
      it depends on the kind of exception:

    

      
What was thrownDoes the container roll back automatically?

      
        
A system exception — a RuntimeException, a RemoteException, or an ErrorYes. The transaction is marked doomed and rolled back. (This is what happened in the bench log.)

        
An application exception — a checked exception your business code threwNo, by default. The container assumes the caller will handle it, so the transaction is left alone.

        
An application exception marked @ApplicationException(rollback = true)Yes. You have told the container that this particular business failure should undo the work.

        
Anything, after you call context.setRollbackOnly()Yes. You have marked the transaction yourself; it will roll back even if this method returns normally.

      
    

    
Why this matters: a checked exception from a validated business rule will
      not undo your work unless you say so. If “insufficient funds” must roll back the transfer,
      either mark it rollback = true or call setRollbackOnly() — do not assume
      it.

  recap
  
One-line summary of everything

  

    
TransactionA group of database steps treated as one: all of it, or none of it.

    
Commit / rollbackCommit makes the changes permanent; rollback quietly undoes them.

    
ACIDAtomicity (all-or-nothing), Consistency (rules hold), Isolation (no peeking), Durability (survives a crash).

    
CMTDeclarative transactions — you label the method and the container draws the boundary. Over 95% of beans.

    
BMTProgrammatic transactions — you call begin() and commit() yourself. Rare, and easy to get wrong.

    
Transaction attributesSix answers to “what shall I do about the caller's transaction?” The default is REQUIRED.

    
REQUIRES_NEWRuns in its own brand-new transaction — the one to use for audit logging that must survive a failure.

    
Lost updateTwo users read the same value, both save, and the later save wipes out the earlier one.

    
Optimistic locking@Version counter, checked at commit. No waiting; the clash is caught at the end.

    
Pessimistic lockingLocks the row on read. Everybody else queues.

    
Singleton locks@Lock(READ) for concurrent getters, @Lock(WRITE) for exclusive changes, @AccessTimeout for the waiting limit.

    
MDBA background listener on a JMS queue. Sender never waits. Clients never call it.

    
CDI eventsA producer fires an event with event.fire(...); any @Observes method in the same application reacts.

    
Security layersApplication (who may call), Transport (TLS encryption), Message (WS-Security signatures).

    
Declarative security@RolesAllowed, @PermitAll, @DenyAll — labels the container enforces for you.

    
Programmatic securityEJBContext.getCallerPrincipal() and isCallerInRole() — for rules that depend on runtime data.

  


  concept
  
Common mistakes to avoid

  

    
❌ The mistake✅ The fix

    
      
Writing the transaction boundary by hand when CMT would doPut @TransactionAttribute on the method and let the container draw the boundary.

      
Marking everything REQUIRES_NEWIt is for work that must survive a caller's rollback. Everywhere else, leave the default REQUIRED.

      
Choosing MANDATORY or NEVER because the words sound strictThey only state whether a transaction must — or must not — already be running. They are not security settings.

      
Relying on optimistic locking where conflicts are constantUnder heavy write conflict, choose pessimistic locking and accept the queueing instead of constant retries.

      
Putting @Lock(LockType.READ) on a method that changes stateAnything that writes needs @Lock(LockType.WRITE). READ means “I only look.”

      
No @AccessTimeout on a WRITE-locked singleton methodSet a sensible timeout, or a waiting thread can hang far longer than the user's patience.

      
Sending the receipt email inside the transactionFire a CDI event or drop a message on a queue, and let the user's page return immediately.

      
Guarding a method with your own if (user.hasRole(...))Use @RolesAllowed. The container enforces it on every call, and it cannot be forgotten when the next method is added.

      
Using a role name the server has never been told about@RolesAllowed("CASHIER") protects nothing if the server has no CASHIER role mapped. The role must exist in the server's security configuration too.

      
Assuming any exception rolls the transaction backOnly unchecked exceptions do, by default. Mark checked ones with @ApplicationException(rollback = true), or call setRollbackOnly().
